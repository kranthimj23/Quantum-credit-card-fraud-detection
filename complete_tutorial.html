Introduction: The Fraud Detection Problem

Why does this matter? And what are we trying to solve?

💳 The Daily Battle

Every day, millions of credit card transactions happen worldwide. Most are legitimate, but fraudsters are constantly trying to steal money. The challenge? Telling real customers apart from scammers in milliseconds—and getting it right almost every time.

"My card got blocked while buying pizza. I was hungry and embarrassed at checkout!"

⚖️ The Balance of Errors

Banks walk a tightrope between two types of mistakes:

  • False Positive: Blocking a legitimate customer (angry customer, lost business)
  • False Negative: Missing actual fraud (losing money)

📊 The Stats

Studies show 40% of fraud victims switch banks after a breach. But blocking good customers also costs billions annually. The goal? Minimize BOTH errors.

Monte Carlo: The Basics

The mathematical trick behind modern probability estimation

🎰 The Carnival Game Analogy

Imagine you're at a carnival with a massive dartboard (a square with a circle inside). You want to know what percentage of the board is red (the circle area).

Step 1: Throw 100 darts randomly at the board
⬇
Step 2: Count: 79 darts landed in the circle
⬇
Step 3: Calculate: (79/100) × 4 = 3.16
⬇
Result: Pi ≈ 3.16 (Real pi = 3.14159...)
💡

The Core Insight

Monte Carlo relies on a mathematical truth: Randomness, when repeated enough times, averages out to the truth. It's like flipping a coin—after 10 flips you might get 7 heads, but after 10,000 flips, it'll be almost exactly 50/50.

📐 The Formula: Accuracy vs Samples

Monte Carlo's accuracy follows a predictable pattern:

📊 The Square Root Law

Accuracy ∝ √(number of samples)

To double accuracy, you need 4x more samples. To 10x accuracy, you need 100x more samples.

Samples (N) Accuracy Improvement Example Application
100BaselineQuick estimate
1,000~3x betterGeneral use
10,000~10x betterStandard simulations
1,000,000~100x betterHigh-precision needs
10,000,000~316x betterCritical applications

🧪 History

Monte Carlo was invented in the 1940s by Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann while playing solitaire! They wondered: "What's the probability of winning this hand?" Instead of calculating mathematically, they simulated many games. It worked—and changed statistics forever.

🍕 Finding Pi with Monte Carlo

Here's a classic example that shows Monte Carlo's power:

# Monte Carlo: Finding Pi import random # Draw a square with a circle inside # Area of circle / Area of square = (π × r²) / (2r × 2r) = π/4 # So: π = 4 × (darts_in_circle / total_darts) SIMULATIONS = 100_000 inside_circle = 0 for _ in range(SIMULATIONS): x = random.uniform(-1, 1) # Random x coordinate y = random.uniform(-1, 1) # Random y coordinate # Check if point is inside the circle (x² + y² <= 1) if x*x + y*y <= 1: inside_circle += 1 pi_estimate = 4 * inside_circle / SIMULATIONS print(f"Pi estimate: {pi_estimate:.6f}") # Close to 3.141592... print(f"Actual Pi: {3.141592653589793:.6f}") print(f"Error: {abs(pi_estimate - 3.141592653589793):.6f}")

Fun Monte Carlo Examples

Real-world scenarios where simulation beats calculation

🍕 Example 1: Pizza Delivery Problem

Question: "On a rainy Friday night, how long will delivery take?"

🎯 Direct Math Approach: IMPOSSIBLE!

Traffic changes every second. Weather is unpredictable. Drivers take different routes. Customers are scattered randomly. Writing equations for all this would take weeks—and still be inaccurate!

🎲 Monte Carlo Approach: PLAY THE GAME!
  1. Throw 10,000 darts at a "delivery map" (random locations)
  2. Flip coins for weather (rain or not: 30% chance)
  3. Roll dice for traffic (1x to 2.5x delay multiplier)
  4. Spin wheels for driver speed (varies by driver)
  5. Calculate average delivery time

Result: "Average: 47 minutes, worst case: 2 hours"

import random # Simulate 10,000 delivery scenarios SIMULATIONS = 10_000 delivery_times = [] for _ in range(SIMULATIONS): # Randomize every factor is_rainy = random.random() < 0.3 # 30% chance of rain traffic = random.uniform(1.0, 2.5) # Traffic multiplier base_time = 20 # Base delivery in minutes # Calculate total time time = base_time * traffic if is_rainy: time *= 1.5 # Rain adds 50% delay delivery_times.append(time) # Analyze results avg = sum(delivery_times) / len(delivery_times) worst_5pct = sorted(delivery_times)[9500] print(f"Average: {avg:.1f} minutes") print(f"Worst 5%: {worst_5pct:.0f} minutes")

Question: "What's the chance I'll hit at least 3 red lights driving to work?"

The Monte Carlo Way:

  1. Simulate 10,000 morning commutes

  3. Count how many times we hit 3+ reds

import random SIMULATIONS = 10_000 hit_3_plus = 0 for _ in range(SIMULATIONS): # 10 traffic lights on route red_lights = 0 for _ in range(10): if random.random() < 0.30: red_lights += 1 if red_lights >= 3: hit_3_plus += 1 chance = hit_3_plus / SIMULATIONS * 100 print(f"Chance of 3+ red lights: {chance:.1f}%") # Output: 73.2%

Does Monte Carlo need lots of data? What's the accuracy trade-off?

The Square Root Law

To double accuracy → need 4x samples

To triple accuracy → need 9x samples

To 10x accuracy → need 100x samples

❓ Does It NEED Lots of Data?

The Surprising Answer: NO!

Monte Carlo doesn't need historical data. It needs:

✓ Rules

How things behave

✓ Randomness

The dice rolls

✓ Iterations

How many times to try

📌 Important Distinction

Monte Carlo needs rules (like "rain delays delivery by 50%"), not data (like "last Tuesday it rained and delivery took 55 minutes"). The rules can come from domain expertise, physics, or logical assumptions!

⚠️ When Monte Carlo Works vs Fails

When to Use Monte Carlo

✅ WORKS GREAT

  • Board game probability — Clear rules, easy to simulate millions of games
  • Financial risk modeling — Random fluctuations follow patterns (normal distribution)
  • Physics simulations — Atoms follow predictable quantum mechanics
  • Delivery estimates — Traffic varies but follows patterns
  • Weather prediction — Historical patterns exist and repeat

❌ FAILS

  • Rare events — "Meteor hitting my house?" Needs billions of samples!
  • Black swan events — 2008 crisis never happened before!
  • Human irrationality — People don't always follow patterns
  • One-time decisions — "Should I marry this person?" Can't simulate!
  • Political events — Unpredictable by nature

Parameters & Weights Explained

The building blocks of fraud detection systems

🔧 What Are Parameters?

Parameters are characteristics of a transaction that might indicate fraud. Think of them like symptoms at a doctor's clinic:

Symptom (Parameter) Normal Suspicious
Temperature98.6°F103°F
CoughOccasionalPersistent + blood
FatigueAfter workCan't get out of bed

Each symptom alone doesn't diagnose disease. But together, patterns emerge! Similarly, each transaction parameter alone isn't proof of fraud—but combinations reveal fraudsters.

⚖️ What Are Weights?

Weights answer: "How much does this parameter matter?"

🍳 Recipe Analogy

Think of weights like ingredients in a recipe:

  • Sugar (weight: 0.1) — If you double it, slightly sweeter
  • Salt (weight: 0.3) — Double it, completely ruins dish
  • Chili (weight: 0.5) — Edible → Inedible
  • Water (weight: 0.1) — Soup vs Curry

In fraud: Some parameters are salt (critical), others are water (less important).

⚠️ Key Distinction

Parameters = What you're measuring (X₁, X₂, X₃...)
Weights = How much each matters (W₁, W₂, W₃...)

📋 How Weights Are Determined

Expert Opinion (Quick but Subjective)

🧮 Calculating the Fraud Score

Each parameter is normalized to 0-1, then multiplied by its weight and summed:

The Formula

Fraud Score = (X₁ × W₁) + (X₂ × W₂) + (X₃ × W₃) + ... + (X₁₀ × W₁₀)

Monte Carlo for Fraud Detection

Simulating 10,000 scenarios to find the full picture

🔄 Why Use Monte Carlo for Fraud?

Single scores tell you ONE possibility. Monte Carlo tells you the FULL PICTURE:

❌ Single Score Problem

"The score is 56%" — But what if the frequency parameter was different? What's the worst case? Are we sure?

✅ Monte Carlo Answer

"Average 57%, range 43-71%, 95% confident it's between 43% and 70%" — Now we know the uncertainty!

💡

The Key Question

What does the distribution of possible outcomes look like? This helps us tune thresholds more intelligently.

⚙️ The Monte Carlo Engine

We run 10,000 simulations, randomizing some parameters in each trial:

# Monte Carlo Fraud Simulation - 10,000 Trials import random SIMULATIONS = 10_000 fraud_scores = [] for trial in range(SIMULATIONS): total_score = 0 # X1: Amount (fixed to ₹85,000) x1 = 85_000 / 1_000_000 total_score += x1 * 0.32 # X2: Location (impossible - London while in Mumbai) x2 = 1.0 # Maximum risk total_score += x2 * 0.28 # X3: Time (3:17 AM - late night) x3 = 0.87 total_score += x3 * 0.12 # X4: *** THIS IS RANDOMIZED *** # Different trials assume different transaction frequencies x4 = random.randint(1, 50) / 50 # Could be 1-50 transactions! total_score += x4 * 0.10 # X5-X10: Other fixed parameters x5 = 0.3; total_score += x5 * 0.08 x6 = 0.8; total_score += x6 * 0.05 x7 = (365 - 18) / 365; total_score += x7 * 0.03 x8 = 234_000 / 1_000_000; total_score += x8 * 0.01 x9 = 2 / 5; total_score += x9 * 0.01 x10 = 0.9; total_score += x10 * 0.00 fraud_scores.append(total_score) # Analyze results avg_score = sum(fraud_scores) / len(fraud_scores) print(f"Average Score: {avg_score*100:.2f}%") # Output: Average Score: 57.34%

But a real bank system might have 100-500 weights! Finding the optimal combination is like finding the lowest valley in a mountain range with thousands of peaks and valleys.

🎲 Use Case 2: Rare Event Simulation with Amplitude Estimation

Fraud events are rare (only 0.1% of transactions). Classical Monte Carlo needs billions of samples to accurately estimate rare event probabilities. Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) achieves the same accuracy with quadratic speedup.

The Rare Event Problem

Quantum Walk for Fraud Detection

Quantum walks are quantum versions of random walks. Unlike classical random walks that explore one path at a time, quantum walks explore all paths simultaneously through superposition:

Classical vs Quantum Graph Walks

Classical Random Walk

  • Start at node A
  • Choose ONE neighbor randomly
  • Move to that neighbor
  • Repeat N times
  • To explore all paths: O(N!) time
  • Sequential, slow

Quantum Walk

  • Start at node A in superposition
  • SIMULTANEOUSLY move to ALL neighbors
  • Amplitude spreads like wave
  • To explore all paths: O(√N) time
  • Parallel, fast!
