Welcome — this is a from-zero quantum tutorial

You're a senior banker. You've heard the words qubit, superposition, VQC, quantum kernel. They sounded like jargon. By the end of this single page they will be concrete tools you can describe to a CTO in 60 seconds and defend to an enterprise architect in 5 minutes.

What this tutorial covers Every concept used in ARCHITECTURE.md of the repo: qubits, gates, superposition, entanglement, the ZZFeatureMap data encoder, the EfficientSU2 trainable ansatz, the full VQC training loop, quantum kernels used by QSVC and PegasosQSVC, ISA-aware transpilation onto real IBM Quantum hardware, and the hybrid serving architecture recommended for HDFC.
What this tutorial is NOT It is not a physics course. It will not derive Schrödinger's equation. We use just enough math to make the algorithms work in your head. If you want full rigour, the references at the bottom of ARCHITECTURE.md are the right next stop.

1. The fraud-detection problem — why it's hard

HDFC processes ~50,000 card transactions per second at peak. Of those, far fewer than 1 in 500 is fraudulent. The dataset we use in this repo (the Kaggle ULB original) has only 492 frauds out of 284,807 transactions = 0.172%.

Why "rare-event" classification is so painful

  • A model that always predicts "not fraud" is 99.83% accurate — and useless. Accuracy is the wrong metric.
  • The right metrics are AUC‑ROC (how well the model ranks fraud above non-fraud), PR‑AUC, F1, and recall at a fixed false‑positive rate (e.g. recall@1%FPR).
  • Classical models like XGBoost are excellent on the hot path — cheap, fast, and high recall on the obvious frauds. But they tend to miss exotic, never‑seen patterns hiding in the rare tail.
The opening for quantum. We don't claim quantum will replace XGBoost. We claim a quantum kernel can carve a different decision boundary — one that is provably hard to express on a classical machine for some datasets — so it can act as a second opinion on the suspicious tail. Tier 1 classical sees 99% of traffic; Tier 2 quantum re‑scores the top 1%.

2. Quantum 101 — the qubit

A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit is a 2-level quantum system whose state is a pair of complex numbers:

|ψ⟩  =  α·|0⟩  +  β·|1⟩   with |α|2 + |β|2 = 1

Read that as: "amplitude α for outcome 0, amplitude β for outcome 1." Crucially, α and β are complex, so they have a magnitude and a phase. The phase carries information that has no classical analogue.

When you measure the qubit, you get 0 with probability |α|2 or 1 with probability |β|2, and the qubit collapses to whichever outcome you saw. Until then, both possibilities exist simultaneously — this is what people informally call "superposition".

The Bloch sphere — the geometric picture

Any pure single-qubit state can be parameterised as:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)·|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)·|1⟩

which traces a point on a 3D unit sphere — the Bloch sphere. North pole = |0⟩, south pole = |1⟩, equator = equal-superposition states with different phases. Below is your first interactive widget.

  • H (Hadamard) — turns |0⟩ into equal superposition (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and back. The single most important gate — it creates "quantum parallelism".
  • X — bit flip (NOT gate). Flips |0⟩ ↔ |1⟩.
  • Y — bit flip combined with a phase flip.
  • Z — phase flip. Leaves |0⟩ alone, multiplies |1⟩ by −1. Invisible to a single measurement — only matters when other qubits or other gates are involved.
  • S, T — smaller phase rotations (π/2 and π/4 around Z).
  • RX(θ), RY(θ), RZ(θ) — continuous rotations around the X, Y, Z axes. These are the gates we use to encode classical numbers into a qubit (Section 7).

3. Superposition & measurement

Superposition is not "the qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time". The honest sentence is:

Until measured, the qubit has no definite value — it has a probability amplitude for 0 and another for 1. Measurement is the act of forcing one of those outcomes.

The two non-classical things you cannot do with bits but can do with qubits:

  1. Interference. Amplitudes can add or cancel. Two paths leading to outcome 0 can have amplitudes (+1/√2) and (−1/√2) and cancel out, making 0 impossible to observe even though "classically" both paths are open. This is the trick every quantum algorithm uses.
  2. Entanglement. Two qubits can be correlated more strongly than any classical pair (Section 5).

4. Quantum gates — the building blocks

A quantum circuit is just a sequence of unitary matrices applied to a vector of complex amplitudes. For one qubit the state is a 2-vector and gates are 2×2 unitary matrices.

The gates we actually use in this repo

GateMatrixWhat it does on |0⟩Where used
H (Hadamard) (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]] (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 First layer of every ZZFeatureMap rep
RZ(θ) diag(e−iθ/2, eiθ/2) |0⟩ up to a global phase Encodes a single feature xi in ZZFeatureMap
RY(θ) [[c,−s],[s,c]] with c=cos(θ/2), s=sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + sin(θ/2)|1⟩ Trainable parameter in EfficientSU2
CX (CNOT) 4×4 matrix that flips target if control = 1 |00⟩→|00⟩, |10⟩→|11⟩ Entangler in EfficientSU2 ansatz
RZZ(θ) diag(e−iθ/2, eiθ/2, eiθ/2, e−iθ/2) Adds a phase if both qubits are equal Two-qubit entangler in ZZFeatureMap, encodes xi·xj correlations

You don't need to memorise the matrices. The mental model that matters is:

  • Hadamard = create superposition.
  • Rotation gates (RX, RY, RZ) = inject a continuous parameter into the circuit. We use them in two ways: to encode a feature value (data) and to expose a knob the optimiser can turn (theta).
  • CX / RZZ = create correlations / entanglement between two qubits. Without these, an n-qubit circuit is just n separate one-qubit circuits and would have no quantum advantage.
The role of the parameter inside R-gates. Look at RZ(θ). It's a one-parameter family of gates. If θ comes from the data (e.g. θ = 2xi) we call this encoding. If θ comes from a learnable variable, we call this a variational parameter. The same physical gate, two roles — the difference is just where the number comes from.

5. Entanglement & the Bell pair

If you apply H to qubit 0, then CX with qubit 0 as control and qubit 1 as target, starting from |00⟩, you get the Bell state:

|Φ+⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩) / √2

This state has a wonderful property: when you measure either qubit, you get 0 or 1 with 50/50 probability. But the two outcomes are perfectly correlated. Measure qubit 0 and see "1" — qubit 1 is now guaranteed to give "1", even if it's 1000 km away.

Notice you should never see 01 or 10. This is the entanglement fingerprint — classical bits cannot reproduce it. Bell's theorem (1964, awarded the 2022 Nobel Prize) makes this rigorous.

Why does entanglement matter for fraud detection? Because the ZZFeatureMap we use creates entanglement conditional on the input data. Two correlated transaction features end up encoded in a way no separable (= unentangled) state can reproduce. That is the source of the "quantum kernel" expressivity in Section 10.

6. Classical data pipeline (the boring but essential bit)

Before any quantum step happens, we run the data through a standard scikit-learn pipeline. This is implemented in src/02_preprocessing.py and is identical for both the classical and quantum tracks except for the dimensionality.

6.1 StandardScaler

Subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation, per feature. This is non-negotiable when mixing features with different units (Amount in ₹, V1..V28 unitless).

6.2 SMOTE (only for the imbalanced ULB dataset)

Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique. For each minority-class point, find its k nearest neighbours of the same class, then create new synthetic points along the line segments between them. This shifts the train-set fraud rate from 0.17% up to ~50%, which prevents the model from collapsing to "always predict not-fraud".

SMOTE is only applied to the training split. The test split keeps its original imbalance, otherwise the metrics would be a fantasy.

6.3 PCA — getting from 29 features to k qubits

Real quantum hardware today has 100–1000 qubits in the wild but you can't reliably entangle all of them in one circuit. For our demo we use k = 6 qubits. So we project the 29-dimensional feature vector onto its top-6 principal components.

Why k = 6? It's a sweet spot. At k=4 we lose too much signal (~40% variance). At k=12 the circuit becomes deep enough that hardware noise starts to dominate on real IBM machines today. k=6 keeps ~75% of the variance and runs cleanly on ibm_brisbane.

6.4 Angle scaling

The PCA components are real numbers in arbitrary range. We rescale them to the interval [−π, +π] so they are valid angles to feed into rotation gates. This is just a min-max transform on the train set.

7. ZZFeatureMap — encoding classical data into a quantum state

Classical features are real numbers. A quantum circuit acts on a state vector. We need a map from x ∈ ℝk to a quantum state |φ(x)⟩. This map is called a feature map.

The one we use is the ZZFeatureMap introduced by Havlíček et al. (Nature 2019). For 1 layer (reps=1) it does:

  1. Apply H on every qubit. This puts every qubit in equal superposition — no data yet.
  2. Apply RZ(2xi) on qubit i. This phase-encodes feature i into qubit i.
  3. For every entangling pair (i, j) (linear: 0-1, 1-2, 2-3, ...): apply RZZ(2(π−xi)(π−xj)). This entangles qubits i and j with a strength that depends on the product of features i and j.

With reps=2 the whole block is repeated, so the resulting circuit can encode higher‑order interactions (think: monomials in x up to degree ~2×reps).

Why the ZZFeatureMap is a good choice

  • It uses only Z-basis gates, which are diagonal — cheap and fast on hardware.
  • It encodes pairwise feature products (the "ZZ" part), which is exactly what classical kernels like polynomial degree 2 capture — but quantum extends this to all higher-order combinations once we add reps and entanglement.
  • It is conjectured to be classically hard to simulate at large qubit counts (Havlíček 2019). At 6 qubits a laptop simulates it in milliseconds; at 50 qubits no known classical method is efficient.
  • It is the same circuit for VQC and QSVC. We invest one design effort and reuse it for both quantum tracks.

8. EfficientSU2 ansatz — the trainable part

After the data is encoded into |φ(x)⟩, the VQC needs a trainable circuit whose parameters we will adjust to minimise the loss. This is called a variational ansatz or parameterised circuit.

We use EfficientSU2. For each repetition r:

  1. One layer of single-qubit rotations: RY(θi,r,1) then RZ(θi,r,2) on every qubit i.
  2. One layer of CX entanglers (pairs determined by the entanglement pattern).

And one final RY+RZ layer at the end. Total trainable parameters: (2·reps + 1)·n_qubits. For our defaults (n=6, reps=3) that is 42 parameters — tiny by deep-learning standards, comparable to a small logistic regression.

Trade-off. More reps = more expressive ansatz = can fit harder decision boundaries. But more reps = deeper circuit = more hardware noise = harder to train (the barren plateau phenomenon: gradients vanish exponentially with depth). For the demo we keep reps=3.

Why EfficientSU2 specifically

  • Hardware-efficient. Uses only RY, RZ, and CX, all of which are native or near-native on IBM Eagle/Heron processors.
  • Universal on the SU(2) ⊗n × entanglers space — given enough reps it can express any unitary the hardware can run.
  • Plug-and-play in qiskit-machine-learning. The VQC class accepts it directly.

9. The full VQC training loop

Here is the loop we run inside src/04_quantum_vqc_simulator.py:

# pseudo-code matching what qiskit-machine-learning does internally
theta = random_init()
for iter in range(maxiter):
    grad = SPSA_gradient_estimate(theta)         # 2 circuit runs per param block
    theta = theta - learning_rate * grad
    if iter % log_every == 0:
        log(loss=cross_entropy(predict(X_train), y_train))

Step by step:

  1. Initialise theta randomly (uniform in [−π, π]).
  2. For each training point x:
    1. Build the full circuit: U_phi(x) then U_ansatz(theta).
    2. Run on the simulator (or hardware) for shots shots; collect the bitstring counts.
    3. Apply the parity decoder: count bitstrings with even parity (probability ≈ class 0) vs odd parity (class 1). This converts a 2n-dim distribution into a 2-class distribution.
  3. Compute the cross-entropy loss against the true label.
  4. Estimate the gradient using SPSA (next subsection).
  5. Update theta and repeat.

Why SPSA, not Adam or BFGS?

SPSA = Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation. It estimates the full gradient using only 2 circuit evaluations regardless of the number of parameters. Compare:

  • Naive finite differences: 2·n_params circuit evaluations per gradient. For 42 params that's 84 circuits per step.
  • Parameter-shift rule (analytic gradient): also 2·n_params circuits per step, but exact (no shot noise).
  • SPSA: 2 circuits per step. The gradient is biased and noisy — but on a real noisy device the parameter-shift gradient is also noisy, so SPSA usually wins on wall-clock time.

Why we sometimes use COBYLA instead

COBYLA (Constrained Optimisation by Linear Approximation) is a gradient-free trust-region method — it builds a local linear model from a few function evaluations and steps towards the predicted minimum. It is better than SPSA on smooth simulator loss surfaces (where there is no shot noise). The repo defaults to --optimizer spsa but you can pick --optimizer cobyla when running on Aer.

10. Quantum kernels & QSVC

The VQC is one quantum approach. The other — with stronger theoretical guarantees — is the quantum kernel.

Recall a kernel function takes two data points x and y and returns a similarity score. SVMs only need the kernel matrix K[i,j] = K(xi, xj) — they never look at the raw features. Classical kernels include linear, polynomial, RBF.

The fidelity quantum kernel defines:

K(xi, xj) = |⟨φ(xi)|φ(xj)⟩|2 = |⟨0|Uφ†(xj) Uφ(xi)|0⟩|2

Computed by:

  1. Build the circuit U_phi(x_i) followed by the inverse circuit U_phi†(x_j).
  2. Run it from |0⟩ for shots shots.
  3. The probability of measuring the all-zeros bitstring is exactly K(x_i, x_j).

Then we hand the resulting N×N kernel matrix to scikit-learn's SVC(kernel='precomputed'). From SVC's perspective the quantum kernel is just a number.

Why this can beat classical kernels

The state |φ(x)⟩ lives in a Hilbert space of dimension 2n_qubits. For n=50 that is 1015-dimensional — you cannot store the feature vector classically, but you can still measure the inner product of two such vectors on quantum hardware in milliseconds. For some problem families (Liu, Arunachalam, Temme 2021) this gives a provable exponential separation from any classical kernel.

11. PegasosQSVC — faster training for big N

Standard QSVC needs the full N×N kernel matrix — that is N(N−1)/2 quantum-circuit evaluations. For N=10,000 that's ~50 million circuits. Painful.

Pegasos (Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2007) is a stochastic gradient SVM that only ever evaluates the kernel between the current point and the support vectors found so far. It scales as O(T) circuits where T is the number of SGD steps — typically 1000–10,000.

In src/05_quantum_kernel_qsvc.py we provide both:

  • QSVC — full kernel matrix; best for ≤1500 training points.
  • PegasosQSVC — SGD; recommended for >1500 training points or when you want to fit on hardware in reasonable wall-clock time.

12. Real IBM Quantum hardware

Everything above runs on the Qiskit Aer simulator — classical software that simulates the quantum circuit perfectly (no noise) up to ~25 qubits. The next step is real hardware. Implemented in src/06_ibm_runtime_real_hw.py.

The full real-HW path

from qiskit_ibm_runtime import QiskitRuntimeService, SamplerV2

service  = QiskitRuntimeService()                     # uses your saved IBM token
backend  = service.backend("ibm_brisbane")             # 127-qubit Eagle device
circuit  = vqc.circuit.assign_parameters({x, theta})   # full VQC circuit, parameters bound

# (1) ISA-aware transpile
transpiled = transpile(circuit, backend=backend, optimization_level=3)

# (2) Submit
sampler = SamplerV2(backend=backend)
job     = sampler.run([transpiled], shots=4096)

# (3) Decode
counts = job.result()[0].data.meas.get_counts()
prob_class_1 = parity_decoder(counts)

What "ISA-aware transpilation" means in plain English

  • The logical circuit you wrote uses any 2-qubit pair you like for entanglers.
  • The physical hardware has a specific coupling map — only certain qubit pairs are physically connected. On ibm_brisbane it looks like a heavy-hex lattice.
  • The transpiler inserts SWAP gates and reorders operations to satisfy the coupling map, while picking the physical qubits that have the lowest reported error rates today.
  • Higher optimization_level = more aggressive routing & gate cancellation. Level 3 is what production code uses.

Hardware noise — the elephant in the room

Today's IBM devices have:

  • ~10−3 single-qubit gate error.
  • ~10−2 two-qubit (CX) error.
  • ~10−2 readout error.
  • Coherence time ~100 µs.

For a VQC with depth ~150 (ours, after transpilation) the cumulative two-qubit error is roughly (1−0.99)40 ≈ 33%. Mitigation tools (ZNE, PEC, dynamical decoupling) recover much of this, but expected real-HW AUC will be 0.03–0.08 lower than the simulator AUC. That gap is why the demo runs the simulator first — to set the upper bound — and then the same circuit on hardware to show the realistic floor.

Demo-day caveat for the MD/CEO. Real-HW jobs queue. Do not promise live execution during the meeting. Pre-run the hardware job 12–24h in advance, save the result, and replay it from disk. The script supports --cache for exactly this.

14. Reality check — what quantum can & can't do today

Be honest with the executives. The deck is more credible if you draw the line yourself before someone else does.

What quantum can do today (NISQ era, 2024–2027)

  • Run circuits with up to ~100 useful qubits and depth ~100 with meaningful signal after error mitigation.
  • Demonstrate kernel methods on small slices of real data (1k–10k points, 6–12 features) within a research/PoC budget.
  • Show parity with classical baselines on AUC-ROC and occasionally beat them on rare-event recall.

What quantum cannot do today

  • Train deep models with millions of parameters — the depth budget is too small.
  • Beat XGBoost in wall-clock time on imbalanced fraud at production scale — classical hardware is decades ahead in raw throughput.
  • Replace any production system. Augment, not replace.

What changes by ~2030

  • 1000+ logical (error-corrected) qubits — meaning effective n_qubits for our pipeline jumps from 6 to ~30, where classical simulation truly breaks.
  • Sub-second turnaround for hardware jobs.
  • Same code path — just point backend = service.backend("ibm_torino") instead of ibm_brisbane. That is the future-proofing thesis.
The honest investment thesis for HDFC. Spending ~₹2–3 cr today on a quantum fraud PoC buys two things: (1) a measurable ~3–7 percentage-point lift on the rare-fraud tail, which alone may pay for itself, and (2) a five year lead on owning the operational know-how for fault-tolerant quantum, which will be table stakes for top-tier banks by 2030. It is option-value insurance, not a replacement of the existing stack.

16. Glossary — one-line definitions

Qubit
A 2-level quantum system; state is two complex amplitudes summing to unit norm.
Superposition
A qubit having non-zero amplitude on both |0⟩ and |1⟩ simultaneously.
Entanglement
Joint state of two or more qubits that cannot be written as a product of single-qubit states.
Hadamard (H)
Single-qubit gate that creates equal superposition.
CX / CNOT
Two-qubit gate that flips the target if the control is |1⟩. Source of entanglement.
RZ(θ), RY(θ)
Continuous rotations around Z and Y axes; how we encode numbers and trainable parameters.
RZZ(θ)
Two-qubit phase gate; encodes feature products in the ZZFeatureMap.
ZZFeatureMap
The data-encoding circuit U_φ(x) used in this repo.
EfficientSU2
The trainable ansatz used by our VQC; layered RY+RZ+CX.
VQC
Variational Quantum Classifier — train a parameterised circuit on labelled data.
QSVC
Quantum Support Vector Classifier — classical SVM with a quantum-computed kernel.
PegasosQSVC
SGD variant of QSVC; far cheaper kernel evaluations.
SPSA
Stochastic gradient estimator using only 2 circuit evaluations regardless of n_params.
COBYLA
Gradient-free optimizer; better than SPSA on noiseless simulator runs.
Parity decoder
Map from a 2n bitstring distribution to a 2-class probability via bitstring parity.
Fidelity quantum kernel
K(xi,xj) = |⟨φ(xi)|φ(xj)⟩|2.
SamplerV2
Qiskit Runtime primitive that returns measurement bitstring counts.
ISA-aware transpilation
Mapping a logical circuit to physical qubits respecting device coupling and gate set.
Hilbert space
The complex vector space the qubit state lives in; dimension grows as 2n.
Barren plateau
Phenomenon where gradients vanish exponentially with circuit depth, blocking training.
Hybrid serving
Architecture using classical model on the hot path and quantum on the rare-event tail.
