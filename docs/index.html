Foundations & Intuition

No prerequisites — build the mental model
CHAPTER 1Intuition

Why Simulate Quantum Systems?

Almost every property of a molecule or material — its color, strength, reactivity, conductivity — is decided by how its electrons arrange themselves. Those electrons obey quantum mechanics, so if we could solve the quantum equations exactly we could design drugs, catalysts, and batteries on a computer instead of in a lab.

The obstacle is the many-body problem. A single electron is easy; but electrons repel one another, so the behavior of each depends on all the others simultaneously. The amount of information needed to describe the joint state grows exponentially with the number of electrons.

A faithful description of $N$ electrons on a grid of $M$ points per dimension needs $\sim M^{3N}$ numbers.

For just 50 electrons this already exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe. This "exponential wall" is why brute force fails and why we need clever methods.

The payoff. Methods that scale gently with system size unlock:

  • Drug discovery — predicting how a molecule binds a protein.
  • Energy — better battery cathodes, catalysts, and (potentially) high-temperature superconductors.
  • Materials — strongly-correlated systems where cheaper methods (like DFT) become unreliable.

Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) is one of the few approaches that delivers high accuracy and scales to interesting sizes — the subject of this course.

CHAPTER 2Intuition

What is Monte Carlo?

Monte Carlo means solving a deterministic problem with random sampling. The classic picture: to find the area of an irregular lake, enclose it in a square of known area, throw darts blindly, and count the fraction that land in the water.

$\text{Area}_{\text{lake}} \approx \text{Area}_{\text{square}} \times \dfrac{\#\,\text{darts in water}}{\#\,\text{darts thrown}}$

More generally, an integral is an average. If we draw samples $x_i$ from a probability density $p(x)$, then

$\displaystyle \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx \;=\; \mathbb{E}_p[f] \;\approx\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i).$

The crucial property: the statistical error of this estimate shrinks like

$\text{error} \;\propto\; \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\,,$

independent of the dimension. A grid-based method in $d$ dimensions needs $M^d$ points to reach a fixed spacing — hopeless for the $3N$-dimensional integrals of quantum mechanics. Monte Carlo sidesteps the curse of dimensionality, which is exactly why it dominates quantum simulation.

The price is randomness: answers come with error bars, and halving the error costs four times the samples. The rest of this course is largely about reducing the prefactor on that $1/\sqrt{N}$.

CHAPTER 3Math

Probability & Statistics Refresher

QMC is applied statistics, so we fix vocabulary precisely.

Random variable & expectation. A random variable $X$ has expectation (mean) and variance

$\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]=\int x\,p(x)\,dx, \qquad \sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}[X]=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2].$

Estimator. The sample mean $\bar{X}_N=\frac1N\sum_i X_i$ estimates $\mu$. It is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\bar X_N]=\mu$. Its own variance is

$\mathrm{Var}[\bar X_N]=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{N}\;\Rightarrow\; \text{standard error}=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}.$

Bias vs. variance. Bias is a systematic offset; variance is random scatter. A good method controls both — much of advanced QMC is about removing biases (timestep, population, nodal) while keeping variance low.

Central Limit Theorem. For large $N$, $\bar X_N$ is approximately Gaussian, $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2/N)$, which justifies reporting results as $\mu \pm \sigma/\sqrt N$ and gives meaning to "1-sigma" error bars.

Correlated samples. QMC samples are not independent (Chapter 8). The effective sample size is $N/(2\tau_{\text{ac}})$ where $\tau_{\text{ac}}$ is the autocorrelation time, so naive error bars underestimate the true uncertainty — fixed by reblocking (Chapter 27).

CHAPTER 4Physics

A Crash Course in Quantum Mechanics

We need just enough quantum mechanics to define what QMC computes.

The wavefunction. The state of $N$ particles is a complex function $\Psi(\mathbf r_1,\dots,\mathbf r_N)$. Its squared magnitude is a probability density:

$|\Psi(R)|^2\,dR = \text{probability of finding the particles in configuration } R.$

This is the bridge to Monte Carlo: $|\Psi|^2$ is a distribution we can sample.

Operators and the Hamiltonian. Observables are operators; the energy operator (Hamiltonian) for electrons is

$\hat H = -\tfrac12\sum_i \nabla_i^2 \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{i

The eigenvalue problem. Stationary states satisfy the time-independent Schrödinger equation

$\hat H \Psi = E\,\Psi.$

The smallest eigenvalue $E_0$ is the ground-state energy — the single most important quantity in chemistry and materials, since structure, bonding, and reaction energetics all derive from it. The whole of VMC and DMC is machinery to estimate $E_0$ (and the corresponding $\Psi_0$) without storing the exponentially large wavefunction explicitly.

II

Mathematical Machinery

The tools every QMC method is built on
CHAPTER 5Math

Linear Algebra for QMC

Quantum mechanics lives in a Hilbert space — a vector space of wavefunctions with an inner product

$\langle \phi|\psi\rangle = \int \phi^*(R)\,\psi(R)\,dR.$

The Hamiltonian $\hat H$ is a Hermitian operator ($\hat H=\hat H^\dagger$), which guarantees real eigenvalues $E_0\le E_1\le\cdots$ and an orthonormal eigenbasis $\{\Psi_n\}$:

$\hat H \Psi_n = E_n \Psi_n,\qquad \langle \Psi_m|\Psi_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}.$

Spectral decomposition. Any state expands as $\Psi=\sum_n c_n\Psi_n$, so

$\langle \Psi|\hat H|\Psi\rangle=\sum_n |c_n|^2 E_n \;\ge\; E_0\,\|\Psi\|^2.$

This single inequality is the seed of the variational principle (Chapter 10) and of imaginary-time projection (Chapter 15): the lowest eigenvalue bounds every expectation value from below, and components with larger $E_n$ can be filtered out.

We will also lean on determinants: antisymmetric fermion wavefunctions are built from Slater determinants, and efficient QMC depends on fast determinant ratio/update formulas (the matrix-determinant lemma, Sherman–Morrison updates).

CHAPTER 6Math

Numerical Integration & the Curse of Dimensionality

Every quantity QMC computes is a high-dimensional integral, e.g. the energy

$E=\dfrac{\int \Psi^*(R)\,\hat H\,\Psi(R)\,dR}{\int |\Psi(R)|^2\,dR},\qquad R\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}.$

Deterministic quadrature (trapezoid, Simpson, Gauss) places a grid of points. With $M$ points per axis the cost is $M^{3N}$ and the error of a product rule falls only polynomially. For $N=100$ electrons, $3N=300$ dimensions — utterly intractable.

Monte Carlo integration replaces the grid with $N_s$ random samples and achieves error $\propto 1/\sqrt{N_s}$ regardless of dimension:

$\int g(R)\,dR \approx \frac{V}{N_s}\sum_{i} g(R_i),\qquad R_i\sim\text{Uniform}.$

The catch: uniform sampling is wildly inefficient because $|\Psi|^2$ is sharply peaked — almost all uniform darts land where the integrand is negligible. The fix is importance sampling (Chapter 7): draw points where they matter. This is the conceptual core that makes QMC practical.

CHAPTER 7Algorithms

Sampling Theory

How do we generate samples from a target density $p(x)$? Three foundational techniques:

1. Inverse-transform. If $u\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)$ then $x=F^{-1}(u)$ has CDF $F$. Exact but needs an invertible CDF — rare in many dimensions.

2. Rejection sampling. Propose from an easy $q(x)$ with $p(x)\le c\,q(x)$, accept with probability $p(x)/(c\,q(x))$. Correct but can reject most proposals in high dimension.

3. Importance sampling. Instead of sampling $p$, sample an easier $q$ and reweight:

$\mathbb{E}_p[f]=\int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx=\int \underbrace{f(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}}_{\text{reweighted}} q(x)\,dx \approx \frac1N\sum_i f(x_i)\frac{p(x_i)}{q(x_i)}.$

The variance is minimized when $q\propto |f|\,p$, motivating QMC's choice to sample $|\Psi_T|^2$ — the density under which the energy estimator is smoothest.

Variance-reduction is the recurring theme: spend samples where the integrand has weight.

In $3N$ dimensions, none of these one-shot schemes suffice on their own; we need a way to walk through configuration space generating correlated-but-correct samples — the Markov chains of Chapter 8.

CHAPTER 8Algorithms

Markov Chains & Detailed Balance

A Markov chain generates a sequence $R_0\to R_1\to R_2\to\cdots$ where each step depends only on the current state via a transition kernel $T(R\to R')$. Under mild conditions the chain forgets its start and settles into a unique stationary distribution $\pi$ satisfying

$\pi(R')=\int \pi(R)\,T(R\to R')\,dR.$

We design $T$ so that $\pi$ is exactly our target $|\Psi_T|^2$. The standard sufficient condition is detailed balance (reversibility):

$\pi(R)\,T(R\to R')=\pi(R')\,T(R'\to R).$

Two more requirements ensure correctness: ergodicity (every region reachable) and aperiodicity.

Autocorrelation. Consecutive samples are correlated. Define the integrated autocorrelation time $\tau_{\text{ac}}$; the variance of the mean is inflated by $2\tau_{\text{ac}}$:

$\mathrm{Var}[\bar X]\approx \dfrac{\sigma^2}{N}\,(1+2\tau_{\text{ac}}).$

We also discard an initial burn-in / equilibration segment before the chain reaches $\pi$. These ideas underpin every Metropolis walk in QMC.

CHAPTER 9Algorithms

The Metropolis–Hastings Algorithm

Metropolis–Hastings (MH) is the universal recipe for building a chain with a prescribed stationary distribution $\pi$. Given current $R$:

  1. Propose $R'$ from a proposal $q(R'\,|\,R)$ (e.g. a small Gaussian move).
  2. Accept with probability
    $A(R\to R')=\min\!\left(1,\ \dfrac{\pi(R')\,q(R\,|\,R')}{\pi(R)\,q(R'\,|\,R)}\right).$
  3. If accepted, move to $R'$; otherwise stay at $R$ (and still record it as a sample).

For a symmetric proposal $q(R'|R)=q(R|R')$ this reduces to the original Metropolis rule $A=\min(1,\pi(R')/\pi(R))$. In VMC, $\pi=|\Psi_T|^2$, so the ratio is

$\dfrac{\pi(R')}{\pi(R)}=\left|\dfrac{\Psi_T(R')}{\Psi_T(R)}\right|^2,$

a cheap ratio — the normalization cancels, which is the whole reason QMC avoids the intractable normalizing integral.

Tuning. Step size controls the acceptance rate: too large ⇒ everything rejected; too small ⇒ slow diffusion. A rule of thumb targets ~50% acceptance to minimize autocorrelation. With MH in hand we can now sample any trial wavefunction — the engine of Part III.

III

Variational Monte Carlo (VMC)

The first real QMC method, end to end
CHAPTER 10Physics

The Variational Principle

The variational principle is the theoretical guarantee that makes VMC trustworthy. For any normalizable trial wavefunction $\Psi_T$,

$E[\Psi_T]=\dfrac{\langle \Psi_T|\hat H|\Psi_T\rangle}{\langle \Psi_T|\Psi_T\rangle}\;\ge\; E_0,$

with equality only when $\Psi_T$ equals the true ground state $\Psi_0$. Proof sketch: expand $\Psi_T=\sum_n c_n\Psi_n$; then $E[\Psi_T]=\sum_n|c_n|^2 E_n/\sum_n|c_n|^2\ge E_0$ since every $E_n\ge E_0$.

Consequence: minimizing the energy over adjustable parameters $\alpha$ can only improve our estimate — we can never undershoot $E_0$. This turns ground-state estimation into an optimization: make $\Psi_T(\alpha)$ as good as possible, and the energy is a rigorous upper bound at every step.

The energy functional is the object we evaluate by Monte Carlo. Rewriting it in a sampleable form (Chapter 12) gives the local-energy estimator; adjusting $\alpha$ to lower it is wavefunction optimization (Chapter 14).

CHAPTER 11Physics

Trial Wavefunctions

VMC is only as good as its trial wavefunction $\Psi_T$. For electrons (fermions) $\Psi_T$ must be antisymmetric under particle exchange, which is achieved with a Slater determinant of single-particle orbitals $\phi_i$:

$D=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\big[\phi_i(\mathbf r_j)\big].$

A bare determinant captures antisymmetry but misses electron correlation. We multiply by a symmetric, positive Jastrow factor $e^{J}$ that explicitly keeps electrons apart:

$\Psi_T(R)=e^{J(R)}\,D^\uparrow D^\downarrow,\qquad J=\sum_{i

This Slater–Jastrow form is the workhorse of QMC. Refinements:

  • Cusp conditions — $u$ is chosen so the divergent Coulomb terms in the local energy cancel as two particles meet (Kato cusps), drastically reducing variance.
  • Backflow — replace $\mathbf r_i$ in the orbitals by collective coordinates $\mathbf r_i+\sum_j \eta(r_{ij})(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j)$, which deforms the nodes and improves DMC.
  • Multi-determinant expansions for systems with strong static correlation.
CHAPTER 12Algorithms

The Local Energy

To evaluate the energy by sampling, rewrite the functional as an average over $|\Psi_T|^2$. Define the local energy

$E_L(R)\equiv \dfrac{\hat H\,\Psi_T(R)}{\Psi_T(R)}.$

Then the variational energy is simply its mean under the density $p(R)=|\Psi_T(R)|^2/\!\int|\Psi_T|^2$:

$E[\Psi_T]=\int E_L(R)\,p(R)\,dR=\mathbb{E}_{p}[E_L]\approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_i E_L(R_i).$

So VMC = sample $|\Psi_T|^2$ with Metropolis, average $E_L$.

Zero-variance principle. If $\Psi_T\to\Psi_0$ (an exact eigenstate), then $\hat H\Psi_0=E_0\Psi_0$ makes $E_L(R)=E_0$ a constant — its variance vanishes. Hence a better trial function gives not only a lower energy but also smaller error bars. The variance of $E_L$ is itself a quality metric and an alternative optimization target.

Computing $E_L$ requires the Laplacian $\nabla^2\Psi_T/\Psi_T$ (kinetic term) plus the potential — all available analytically (or via autodiff) for Slater–Jastrow forms.

CHAPTER 13Algorithms

The VMC Algorithm End-to-End

Putting Parts I–II together, the full VMC loop is:

  1. Initialize walkers at random electron positions $R_0$ and pick parameters $\alpha$.
  2. Equilibrate — run Metropolis moves (Chapter 9) sampling $|\Psi_T|^2$ and discard the burn-in.
  3. Accumulate — continue the walk; at each step evaluate $E_L(R_i)$ and accumulate it (and any other observables).
  4. Estimate — report the mean energy with an error bar:
    $\bar E=\frac1N\sum_i E_L(R_i),\qquad \delta E=\dfrac{\sigma_{E_L}}{\sqrt{N/(2\tau_{\text{ac}})}}.$

Blocking analysis. Because samples are autocorrelated, naive error bars are too small. Group the series into blocks of growing size and watch the block-averaged error grow then plateau; the plateau is the correct $\delta E$ (Chapter 27 makes this rigorous with reblocking/jackknife).

VMC gives a rigorous variational energy and a fully optimized $\Psi_T$ — which becomes the launch point and importance function for the more accurate Diffusion Monte Carlo of Part IV.

CHAPTER 14Algorithms

Wavefunction Optimization

The parameters $\alpha$ (Jastrow coefficients, orbital and backflow parameters, determinant weights) must be tuned to minimize energy or variance.

Energy vs. variance minimization. Minimizing $\mathbb{E}[E_L]$ targets the physical quantity directly; minimizing $\mathrm{Var}[E_L]$ is more stable but optimizes a proxy. Modern practice often blends both.

Gradients from sampling. The energy gradient has a convenient estimator

$\partial_\alpha E = 2\,\Big\langle (\partial_\alpha \ln\Psi_T)\,(E_L-\langle E_L\rangle)\Big\rangle.$

Methods, in increasing power:

  • Steepest descent — simple but slow and noise-sensitive.
  • Stochastic Reconfiguration (SR) — natural-gradient step preconditioned by the overlap (Fisher) matrix $S_{ij}=\langle O_i O_j\rangle-\langle O_i\rangle\langle O_j\rangle$, with $O_i=\partial_{\alpha_i}\ln\Psi_T$:
    $\Delta\alpha = -\eta\,S^{-1}\,\partial_\alpha E.$
  • Linear method — solves a generalized eigenvalue problem in the tangent space; converges in remarkably few iterations.
  • Newton / Hessian approaches for the final polishing.

For neural-network ansätze (Chapter 29) SR reappears as natural gradient / KFAC, which is essential to train millions of parameters.

IV

Diffusion Monte Carlo & Projector Methods

Systematically reaching the exact ground state
CHAPTER 15Physics

Imaginary-Time Evolution

VMC is capped by the quality of $\Psi_T$. Projector methods remove that cap by filtering the trial state toward the exact ground state. The key trick is to evolve in imaginary time $\tau=it$:

$|\Psi(\tau)\rangle=e^{-(\hat H-E_T)\tau}\,|\Psi_T\rangle.$

Expand in eigenstates, $|\Psi_T\rangle=\sum_n c_n|\Psi_n\rangle$:

$|\Psi(\tau)\rangle=\sum_n c_n\,e^{-(E_n-E_T)\tau}\,|\Psi_n\rangle.$

Every component decays at a rate set by its energy. Since $E_0$ is smallest, all excited states decay faster than the ground state. As $\tau\to\infty$ only the ground state survives:

$\lim_{\tau\to\infty}|\Psi(\tau)\rangle \propto |\Psi_0\rangle,\quad\text{provided } c_0\neq 0.$

Tuning the reference energy $E_T\to E_0$ keeps the norm finite. The remaining problem is purely computational: how to apply $e^{-\hat H\tau}$ without building exponentially large matrices. The answer (Chapter 16) is that imaginary-time evolution looks exactly like diffusion — which we can simulate with random walkers.

CHAPTER 16Physics

The Diffusion–Drift–Branching Picture

Write the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation in position space:

$-\dfrac{\partial \Psi}{\partial \tau}= -\tfrac12\nabla^2\Psi + (V(R)-E_T)\,\Psi.$

This is a diffusion equation with a source/sink term. The two pieces have direct stochastic meaning:

  • The $-\tfrac12\nabla^2\Psi$ term is ordinary diffusion — simulated by Gaussian random walks of "walkers".
  • The $(V-E_T)\Psi$ term is a rate term — it multiplies/removes walker weight (branching).

Formally the short-time propagator factorizes via a Green's function

$G(R\to R',\delta\tau)\approx \underbrace{(2\pi\delta\tau)^{-3N/2}e^{-\frac{|R'-R|^2}{2\delta\tau}}}_{\text{diffusion}}\;\underbrace{e^{-(\frac{V(R)+V(R')}{2}-E_T)\delta\tau}}_{\text{branching}} ,$

valid to $O(\delta\tau^2)$ (the source of timestep error, removed by extrapolating $\delta\tau\to0$). Iterating this short-time step many times realizes $e^{-\hat H\tau}$ as a population of diffusing, branching walkers — the essence of DMC.

CHAPTER 17Algorithms

Importance Sampling in DMC

Naive branching on $V(R)$ is catastrophic: the Coulomb potential diverges, so walker weights explode near nuclei. The cure is importance sampling — evolve the product $f(R,\tau)=\Psi_T(R)\,\Psi(R,\tau)$ instead of $\Psi$ itself.

Substituting $f$ into the imaginary-time equation yields three terms: diffusion, a new drift term, and branching governed by the (bounded) local energy rather than the raw potential:

$-\partial_\tau f = -\tfrac12\nabla^2 f + \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\mathbf v_D\,f\big) + \big(E_L(R)-E_T\big)f,$
$\text{drift velocity}\quad \mathbf v_D(R)=\nabla \ln|\Psi_T(R)| = \dfrac{\nabla\Psi_T}{\Psi_T}.$

The drift pushes walkers toward regions where $|\Psi_T|$ is large, and branching now uses the smooth $E_L$ (which is constant for a perfect $\Psi_T$, killing the fluctuations). The walker population sampled is $f=\Psi_T\Psi_0$, giving the mixed estimator

$E_0=\dfrac{\langle \Psi_T|\hat H|\Psi_0\rangle}{\langle \Psi_T|\Psi_0\rangle}=\langle E_L\rangle_f,$

which is exact for the energy even though $f\neq|\Psi_0|^2$.

CHAPTER 18Algorithms

The Branching (Birth/Death) Process

The branching factor over one step,

$b(R\to R')=\exp\!\Big[-\Big(\tfrac{E_L(R)+E_L(R')}{2}-E_T\Big)\delta\tau\Big],$

controls walker reproduction. Practically, a walker with weight is either:

  • cloned (born) if it sits in a low-$E_L$, favorable region ($b>1$), or
  • killed (dies) if it sits in a high-$E_L$ region ($b<1$).

Stochastic reconfiguration of the population keeps the integer walker count manageable (e.g. duplicate $\lfloor b+u\rfloor$ copies, $u\sim$Uniform).

Population-control bias. Adjusting $E_T$ each step to hold the population steady introduces a small bias $\propto 1/N_{\text{walkers}}$; it is removed by using many walkers and/or extrapolating. Keeping the population stable while unbiased is a central engineering concern:

$E_T(\tau+\delta\tau)=E_T(\tau)+\dfrac{\kappa}{\delta\tau}\ln\!\dfrac{N_{\text{target}}}{N(\tau)}.$

Balanced birth/death plus drift makes the walker distribution converge to $f=\Psi_T\Psi_0$.

CHAPTER 19Algorithms

The Full DMC Algorithm

The complete fixed-node DMC procedure:

  1. Start from VMC — take the optimized $\Psi_T$ and a population of walkers sampled from $|\Psi_T|^2$.
  2. Drift + diffuse each walker: $R'=R+\mathbf v_D(R)\,\delta\tau+\boldsymbol{\xi}$, with $\boldsymbol\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,\delta\tau)$.
  3. Accept/reject (a Metropolis step restores detailed balance and reduces timestep error).
  4. Branch using $b(R\to R')$; adjust $E_T$ for population control.
  5. Accumulate $\langle E_L\rangle$ once equilibrated.

Two systematic errors to control:

  • Timestep error $O(\delta\tau)$ — run several $\delta\tau$ and extrapolate to $\delta\tau\to0$.
  • Population-control bias $O(1/N_w)$ — use large populations.

Mixed vs. pure estimators. The energy is exact from the mixed estimator, but other observables $\hat A$ that don't commute with $\hat H$ need correction: $\langle A\rangle_{\text{pure}}\approx 2\langle A\rangle_{\text{mixed}}-\langle A\rangle_{\text{VMC}}$, or forward-walking/future-walking techniques. Aside from the fixed-node approximation (Part V), DMC is otherwise exact.

CHAPTER 20Advanced

Green's Function & Reptation Monte Carlo

DMC is one realization of projector Monte Carlo; two important relatives:

Green's Function Monte Carlo (GFMC). Rather than a short-time Gaussian, GFMC samples the exact Green's function $G=\langle R'|(\hat H-E_T)^{-1}|R\rangle$ (or $e^{-\hat H\tau}$) using domain Green's function / Trotter-free schemes, eliminating timestep error at the cost of more complex sampling. It was historically central for lattice models and liquid helium.

Reptation Monte Carlo (RMC). Samples whole imaginary-time paths ("reptiles") with a bounce algorithm, moving one end at a time like a reptating polymer. Its advantages:

  • Gives pure (not mixed) estimators directly, so static properties and densities are unbiased.
  • Avoids population-control bias entirely (fixed number of paths).

The trade-off is longer autocorrelation along the path. Choosing among DMC / GFMC / RMC depends on whether you need energies (DMC), unbiased observables (RMC), or freedom from timestep error (GFMC). All share the same fixed-node limitation addressed next.

The Sign Problem & Advanced Physics

The hardest open challenges in the field
CHAPTER 21Physics

Fermions and the Sign Problem

Electrons are fermions: the Pauli principle demands that exchanging any two flips the sign of the wavefunction,

$\Psi(\dots,\mathbf r_i,\dots,\mathbf r_j,\dots)=-\,\Psi(\dots,\mathbf r_j,\dots,\mathbf r_i,\dots).$

So $\Psi$ has both positive and negative regions. But DMC interprets the walker density as a probability, which must be non-negative. Trying to represent a signed function with positive walkers leads to catastrophe:

$\text{signal/noise}\;\propto\; e^{-(E_F-E_B)\tau}\to 0,$

where $E_F-E_B$ is the (positive) gap between the fermionic and bosonic ground states. The positive and negative populations grow toward the bosonic ground state and their difference — the fermionic signal — is swamped by noise that grows exponentially in system size and imaginary time.

This is the sign problem, proven NP-hard in general (Troyer & Wiese, 2005). It is the central obstacle of computational many-fermion physics; the rest of Part V is about taming it.

CHAPTER 22Advanced

The Fixed-Node Approximation

The dominant practical cure is the fixed-node approximation. The nodes of a wavefunction are the surface where it vanishes and changes sign:

$\mathcal{N}=\{R:\Psi_T(R)=0\}.$

We impose the nodal surface of the trial function $\Psi_T$ on the projected state and forbid walkers from crossing it (any move across a node is rejected). Within each nodal pocket $\Psi$ has a fixed sign, so the simulation is sign-problem-free and exactly solves the Schrödinger equation with the boundary condition $\Psi=0$ on $\mathcal N$.

Variational property. The fixed-node energy is a rigorous upper bound to $E_0$ and becomes exact if the nodes are exact:

$E_{\text{FN}}\ge E_0,\qquad E_{\text{FN}}=E_0 \iff \mathcal N=\mathcal N_{\text{exact}}.$

The only remaining error is the nodal error — typically a few percent of the correlation energy, far better than mean-field methods. Improving nodes (via backflow, multi-determinant, or neural ansätze) is therefore the key to higher accuracy, which is why Chapter 33 exists.

CHAPTER 23Advanced

Beyond Fixed-Node

Fixed-node trades the sign problem for a controllable bias. Several methods aim to reduce or remove that bias:

  • Released-node DMC — let walkers cross nodes but carry a sign, and extract the fermionic signal before the noise dominates. Exact in principle, but only feasible for small systems because the signal decays exponentially.
  • Fixed-phase — the complex-wavefunction generalization (needed in magnetic fields / for complex orbitals): fix the phase of $\Psi_T$ rather than its nodes.
  • Nodal optimization — systematically improve $\mathcal N$ by optimizing backflow, multi-determinant expansions, or neural networks within VMC before running DMC.
  • Constrained-path / phaseless — the same philosophy applied in the auxiliary-field formulation (Chapter 24), constraining walker determinants rather than positions.

The frontier (Chapter 35) replaces hand-built trial nodes with machine-learned ones and even uses quantum computers to supply better constraints — steadily shrinking the last systematic error of QMC.

CHAPTER 24Advanced

Auxiliary-Field QMC (AFQMC)

AFQMC works in second quantization and is the method of choice for solids and lattice models. The two-body interaction is decoupled into one-body problems coupled to fluctuating fields via the Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation:

$e^{-\delta\tau\,\hat H_2}=\int d\mathbf x\;p(\mathbf x)\;e^{-\delta\tau\,\hat h_1(\mathbf x)},$

turning interacting electrons into non-interacting electrons moving in random auxiliary fields $\mathbf x$. Each field configuration propagates a Slater determinant (cheap, via matrix algebra); averaging over fields restores the interaction.

The phase problem. The fields are generally complex, so walker determinants acquire complex phases — AFQMC's version of the sign problem. The phaseless approximation (Zhang & Krakauer) constrains the random walk using a trial state $|\Psi_T\rangle$ to keep weights real and positive, analogous to fixed-node. Accuracy hinges on $|\Psi_T\rangle$, motivating multi-determinant and even quantum-computer trial states (Chapter 35). AFQMC scales as a low polynomial in system size and parallelizes superbly.

CHAPTER 25Advanced

Path-Integral & Finite-Temperature QMC

Everything so far targeted the ground state ($T=0$). At finite temperature we want thermal averages weighted by $e^{-\beta\hat H}$, $\beta=1/k_BT$. Feynman's path integral maps a quantum particle at temperature $T$ onto a classical ring polymer of $P$ "beads" connected by springs:

$Z=\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta\hat H}=\oint \mathcal D R(\tau)\;e^{-S[R(\tau)]},\qquad \beta=P\,\delta\tau.$

Path-Integral Monte Carlo (PIMC) samples these closed paths with Metropolis moves. It is essentially exact for bosons (e.g. superfluid $^4$He) and even captures Bose–Einstein condensation through permutation moves — efficiently sampled by the worm algorithm, which opens the paths to update winding/permutation sectors.

For fermions at finite $T$, antisymmetry under permutation reintroduces the sign problem (restricted-PIMC / fixed-node-in-$\beta$ approximations are used). PIMC is the natural framework for thermodynamics, quantum solids/liquids, and warm dense matter.

CHAPTER 26Advanced

Lattice Models & Continuous-Time QMC

Condensed-matter physics often abstracts materials into lattice models — the Hubbard model (itinerant electrons with on-site repulsion $U$) and the Heisenberg model (interacting spins) — that capture magnetism, Mott transitions, and (possibly) high-$T_c$ superconductivity.

Specialized QMC algorithms dominate here:

  • Stochastic Series Expansion (SSE) — expand $e^{-\beta\hat H}$ as a power series and sample the operator strings; extremely efficient and sign-problem-free for unfrustrated spin models.
  • World-line / loop algorithms — global cluster updates that beat critical slowing down.
  • Continuous-Time QMC (CT-QMC) — works directly in the $\delta\tau\to0$ limit (no Trotter error) by sampling diagrammatic expansions in continuous imaginary time. CT-QMC impurity solvers (CT-INT, CT-HYB) are the engine inside Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT), the standard tool for strongly-correlated materials.

Frustrated and doped-fermionic lattices still suffer the sign problem, linking back to the open challenges of Chapters 21–23 and the research directions of Part VII.

CHAPTER 27Practice

Error Analysis & Statistics in Practice

A QMC number without a trustworthy error bar is meaningless. The two error types:

  • Statistical — random scatter, $\propto 1/\sqrt N$, reducible by sampling longer.
  • Systematic — biases (timestep, population, nodal, basis set) that do not shrink with $N$ and must be extrapolated away.

Autocorrelation & reblocking. Because Markov samples are correlated, the naive error underestimates the truth. Reblocking (Flyvbjerg–Petersen) averages the series into blocks of size $b$; the estimated error rises with $b$ and plateaus once blocks are uncorrelated:

$\delta E(b)=\dfrac{\sigma_b}{\sqrt{N_b-1}}\xrightarrow{b\ \text{large}}\ \text{true error}.$

Resampling. Jackknife (leave-one-block-out) and bootstrap (resample with replacement) propagate errors correctly through nonlinear functions of averages (variances, ratios, extrapolations).

Best practice: report value, statistical error, and a clear statement of each extrapolation ($\delta\tau\to0$, $N_w\to\infty$, basis $\to$ complete) with its residual systematic uncertainty.

CHAPTER 28Practice

High-Performance & Parallel QMC

QMC is famously "embarrassingly parallel": walkers are largely independent, so $P$ processors give nearly $P\times$ throughput. This makes QMC a natural fit for GPUs and the largest supercomputers.

Scaling concerns in real codes:

  • Load balancing — branching makes walker counts uneven across ranks; periodic redistribution keeps cores busy.
  • Memory & orbitals — B-spline (blip) representations of orbitals trade memory for fast $O(1)$ evaluation.
  • Fast updates — Sherman–Morrison / matrix-determinant-lemma updates give determinant ratios in $O(N^2)$ after single-electron moves.
  • Mixed precision & GPU kernels — modern neural-QMC codes run in JAX/PyTorch on accelerators.

Production packages: QMCPACK (open-source, GPU, solids & molecules), CASINO (continuum VMC/DMC), QWalk, TurboRVB (VMC/lattice), and ALF/ALPS for lattice AFQMC/SSE. Reproducibility (seeds, equilibration, extrapolation protocols) matters as much as raw speed.

CHAPTER 29Frontier

Machine-Learning Wavefunctions (Neural-Network QMC)

Since 2017, neural networks have become the most expressive trial wavefunctions ever used. A deep network parameterizes $\Psi_\theta(R)$ with millions of weights, trained by VMC energy minimization (natural gradient / KFAC).

Key requirement: fermionic antisymmetry. Architectures enforce it with generalized (permutation-equivariant) Slater determinants:

$\Psi_\theta(R)=\sum_k \det\!\big[\phi^k_i(\mathbf r_j;\,\{\mathbf r\})\big],$

where each orbital depends on all electrons through an equivariant network (built-in backflow).

Landmark models:

  • NQS (Carleo & Troyer, Science 2017) — RBM states for lattice spins.
  • FermiNet (Pfau et al., 2020) and PauliNet (Hermann et al., 2020) — near-exact molecular energies in the continuum, no fixed nodes.
  • Psiformer (2023) — transformer/self-attention ansatz scaling to larger molecules.

Open challenges: cost per step (network Laplacians), scaling to solids and 100+ electrons, excited states, and transferability across geometries (amortized training). This is the most active accuracy frontier in QMC — see Chapter 33.

CHAPTER 30Frontier

QMC Meets Quantum Computing

QMC and quantum computers are complementary, not rivals.

QMC as a benchmark. Because QMC gives near-exact energies for many systems classically, it validates results from noisy quantum hardware and calibrates claims of quantum advantage.

Hybrid quantum-classical QMC. The headline result (Huggins, Lee, et al., Nature 2022) uses a quantum computer to provide a better trial state / overlap inside AFQMC, statistically de-biasing the phaseless constraint beyond what classical trial states allow:

$\langle \Psi_Q|\phi_w\rangle \ \text{estimated on hardware} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{tighter constraint, smaller bias.}$

Open questions: the shot/measurement overhead of estimating overlaps, robustness to NISQ noise, and whether a provable, scalable advantage survives at large system sizes.

The likely near-term role: quantum devices supply hard-to-compute ingredients (trial states, overlaps) while classical QMC does the heavy statistical lifting — bridging today's hardware to tomorrow's fault-tolerant machines.

Improving the QMC Algorithm

CHAPTER 31Research

Where QMC Still Falls Short

Despite its accuracy, QMC has four structural limitations that bound what it can do. Naming them precisely tells us where to invest research effort:

  1. The fermion sign problem — variance grows exponentially with system size and inverse temperature for general fermionic / frustrated systems (NP-hard, Troyer–Wiese 2005).
  2. Trial-wavefunction / nodal bias — fixed-node DMC is only as accurate as the nodes of $\Psi_T$; AFQMC only as good as its constraint trial state.
  3. Statistical efficiency — the unavoidable $1/\sqrt N$ tax, inflated further by long autocorrelation times.
  4. Wall-clock & scaling cost — optimization and the $\delta\tau\to0$, $N_w\to\infty$, basis-set extrapolations multiply the expense.

Chapters 32–36 address each in turn, citing the Q1 literature. The overarching modern theme: reframe these as optimization and representation problems, and bring machine learning and quantum hardware to bear.

CHAPTER 32Research

Attacking the Sign Problem

The sign problem can't be solved universally, but it can be mitigated for important Hamiltonian classes and the constraints that tame it can be made tighter.

  • Basis / gauge optimization. The sign problem is representation-dependent — a local basis rotation can reduce or eliminate it. Hangleiter et al. (Sci. Adv. 6, eabb8341, 2020) cast "easing" the sign problem as an explicit optimization over local transformations.
  • Phaseless / constrained-path AFQMC. Zhang & Krakauer (PRL 90, 136401, 2003) control the walker phase; the frontier is replacing the mean-field constraint trial state with multi-determinant or neural states to shrink the bias (Motta & Zhang review, WIREs Comput. Mol. Sci. 2018).
  • Adaptive / self-learning constraints that learn an effective sign-free model on the fly.
  • Quantum-assisted de-biasing (Chapter 35).
Practical rule: before fighting the sign numerically, ask whether a change of basis removes it.
CHAPTER 33Research

Better Wavefunctions = Better Nodes

In fixed-node DMC the only remaining error is the node, so improving $\Psi_T$ is the single highest-impact lever — and where the field has moved fastest since 2017.

Neural-network ansätze. Carleo & Troyer (Science 355, 602, 2017) launched neural quantum states; FermiNet (Pfau et al., Phys. Rev. Research 2, 033429, 2020) and PauliNet (Hermann et al., Nat. Chem. 12, 891, 2020) reach near-exact molecular energies; Psiformer (von Glehn et al., 2023) scales attention-based ansätze to larger systems.

Classical upgrades that still pay. Backflow transformations and multi-determinant expansions systematically deform nodes; energy-minimization via the linear method (Umrigar et al., PRL 98, 110201, 2007) optimizes them robustly.

Open targets: neural ansätze for solids and 100+ electrons, cheaper network Laplacians, excited states, and transferable/amortized models across geometries (DeepErwin, Scherbela et al., Nat. Comput. Sci. 2022).

CHAPTER 34Research

Statistical Efficiency

Even with perfect nodes we pay the $1/\sqrt N$ tax; this chapter lowers the prefactor and the autocorrelation time $\tau_{\text{ac}}$.

  • Better optimizers. Stochastic Reconfiguration / natural gradient (Sorella, PRB 64, 024512, 2001) and the linear method converge in far fewer, far-less-noisy iterations than plain SGD — essential for million-parameter neural ansätze (where KFAC makes natural gradient affordable).
  • Variance reduction. Zero-variance / zero-bias estimators (Assaraf & Caffarel, PRL 83, 4682, 1999) and improved estimators for forces and densities.
  • Correlated sampling for energy differences (reaction energies, forces), whose variance is far smaller than that of absolute energies.
  • Decorrelation. Smarter proposals (all-electron updates, Langevin/HMC-style moves) shorten $\tau_{\text{ac}}$ and raise the effective sample size $N/(2\tau_{\text{ac}})$.
"Improving QMC" often means improving the optimizer and estimators, not the sampler.
CHAPTER 35Research

Cost, Scaling & the Quantum Frontier

Making accurate QMC affordable proceeds on two complementary fronts — classical scaling and quantum assistance.

Classical scaling.

  • Accelerators: GPU/HPC ports (QMCPACK, Kim et al., J. Phys.: Condens. Matter 30, 195901, 2018) and JAX/PyTorch neural-QMC.
  • Reduced-scaling AFQMC via low-rank / tensor-hypercontraction factorizations of the two-electron integrals, pushing larger basis sets.
  • Amortization / transfer learning so each new geometry is cheap (DeepErwin, 2022).

Quantum frontier. QC-QMC (Huggins, Lee, et al., Nature 603, 416, 2022) uses a quantum computer to supply trial-state overlaps that statistically unbias the classical constraint, in principle removing nodal bias beyond classical reach. Open issues: measurement (shot) overhead, NISQ noise robustness, and scalability of any provable advantage.

CHAPTER 36Practice

A Practitioner's Improvement Checklist

When asked "how do we improve our QMC pipeline?", work top-down:

  1. Diagnose the dominant error — statistical (run longer / reduce variance), nodal (improve $\Psi_T$), timestep/population (DMC controls), or sign (representation/constraint)?
  2. Upgrade the ansatz before the sampler — Jastrow/backflow → multi-determinant → neural network, as budget allows.
  3. Switch to natural-gradient optimization (SR / linear method / KFAC) if optimization is the bottleneck.
  4. Use correlated sampling for any energy difference you care about.
  5. Profile and parallelize walkers across GPUs; consider low-rank integral factorizations for AFQMC.
  6. Re-examine the representation for sign-problem-limited systems before brute-forcing more samples.
  7. Scope a quantum-assisted step only after classical levers (1–6) are exhausted.
Most real-world QMC gains come from a better ansatz and a better optimizer — in that order.
