Phase 1 — Foundations

What UPI is, who the players are, and how money conceptually moves.
CHAPTER 1

The UPI Ecosystem and Its Participants

UPI (Unified Payments Interface) is a real-time, account-to-account payment system operated by NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India). Unlike cards, there is no card number and no scheme like Visa in the middle — NPCI itself is the central switch that routes messages between banks.

The participants you must know cold:

  • NPCI — the central router, rule-maker, and settlement operator. Every UPI message passes through it.
  • Issuer / Remitter bank — the payer's bank; it debits the customer's account. Its issuer switch is the system this course teaches you to build.
  • Beneficiary / Acquirer bank — the payee's bank; it credits the receiving account (or merchant).
  • PSP bank (Payment Service Provider) — the bank that connects a customer-facing app to NPCI and owns the VPA handle.
  • TPAP (Third-Party Application Provider) — apps like GPay, PhonePe, Paytm. They are not banks; they ride on a sponsor PSP bank.
  • Customer & Merchant — the end parties.
flowchart LR C[Customer
UPI app] --> T[TPAP
e.g. GPay] T --> P[PSP Bank
payer side] P --> N[NPCI
central switch] N --> I[Issuer Bank
issuer switch + CBS] N --> B[Beneficiary Bank
credit leg] B --> M[Merchant / Payee]

The 4-party model (customer, payer PSP=issuer, NPCI, beneficiary) becomes a 5-party model when the app is a TPAP riding on a separate PSP bank — which is the common case today. A single real payment can involve: TPAP → PSP bank → NPCI → issuer bank → NPCI → beneficiary bank.

Real-world example: You pay a chaiwala with GPay. GPay (TPAP) sends the request via its sponsor PSP (e.g. Axis). NPCI routes a debit to your bank (issuer switch verifies your UPI PIN and debits you), then routes a credit to the chaiwala's bank. Total elapsed: ~2–3 seconds across 6 systems.

Regulatory frame: RBI regulates under the PSS Act; NPCI publishes the UPI Procedural Guidelines, Operating Circulars, and API specifications that your switch must comply with. Banks join UPI by signing NPCI's agreement and passing certification (Chapter 14).

CHAPTER 2

VPAs, PSPs, TPAPs, and Handles

A VPA (Virtual Payment Address) like alice@okhdfcbank is an alias that hides the real account number. Its anatomy:

PartExampleMeaning
Local partaliceChosen by user/app; unique within the handle
Handle@okhdfcbankRegistered by a PSP bank with NPCI; identifies which PSP resolves this VPA

Resolution: NPCI keeps a mapper of handle → PSP bank. When someone pays alice@okhdfcbank, NPCI routes an address-resolution request to that PSP, which returns the underlying account + IFSC (masked from the payer). The payer never learns the account number.

CHAPTER 3

UPI Transaction Types and Rails

Every UPI payment is either a Pay (push) — payer initiates — or a Collect (pull) — payee requests and payer approves. On top of these two primitives, UPI builds many rails:

RailTypeTypical useNotes
P2P PayPushSend money to a friendDefault flow; VPA/mobile/QR
P2M Pay (QR / Intent)PushMerchant paymentsStatic QR (fixed VPA) vs dynamic QR (amount+ref embedded); app-to-app intent on mobile
CollectPullBill requestsHeavily restricted now due to fraud; limits apply
Mandates (UPI Autopay)Pull, pre-authorizedSubscriptions, SIPsOne-time PIN at creation; executions are PIN-less within limits
UPI LitePush, on-deviceSmall offline-ish paymentsBalance held in device wallet; no PIN per txn
Credit line on UPIPushPay from pre-sanctioned creditIssuer exposes a credit account over UPI
International UPIPushUPI abroad (e.g. Singapore PayNow link)Cross-border corridor via NPCI International

Initiation methods matter for your acquirer design: QR scan, deep link / intent (upi://pay?pa=...&am=...), in-app VPA entry, and NFC (UPI Tap). A dynamic merchant QR encodes payee VPA, amount, merchant ref and a signature.

Limits and codes: per-transaction caps (generally ₹1 lakh; higher for specific MCCs like hospitals/education/IPO), MCC (merchant category codes) on P2M, and zero-MDR policy for UPI P2M — which shapes the business case of an acquiring switch.

Example: A Netflix subscription = UPI Autopay mandate: created once with PIN, then executed monthly by the merchant's PSP as a pre-approved debit, notified to the customer 24h in advance.

CHAPTER 4

End-to-End Transaction Message Flow

This is the single most important diagram in UPI. A P2P Pay via a TPAP, hop by hop:

sequenceDiagram autonumber participant App as Payer App (TPAP) participant PSP as Payer PSP Switch participant NPCI participant ISS as Issuer Switch + CBS participant BEN as Beneficiary Bank App->>PSP: Initiate pay (amount, payee VPA, PIN credential block) PSP->>NPCI: ReqPay (signed XML) NPCI->>ISS: ReqPay — DEBIT leg ISS->>ISS: Verify PIN (HSM), limits, balance ISS->>ISS: Debit account in CBS ISS-->>NPCI: RespPay (debit SUCCESS) NPCI->>BEN: ReqPay — CREDIT leg BEN->>BEN: Validate + credit account BEN-->>NPCI: RespPay (credit SUCCESS) NPCI-->>PSP: RespPay (final status) PSP-->>App: Payment success + RRN

Key properties of this flow:

  • Debit-first: the payer is debited before the credit is attempted. If credit fails, money must be reversed or the credit retried — this is where recon (Ch. 17) earns its keep.
  • Asynchronous request/callback: every ReqPay gets an HTTP 200 ACK immediately; the real answer arrives later as a separate RespPay callback. Your switch must correlate them.
  • Deemed approval: if the beneficiary doesn't answer in time, NPCI may treat the credit as "deemed" success — the beneficiary bank must honour it and fix its books later.
  • The RRN (retrieval reference number) is the customer-visible ID that ties all legs together.
Why customers see "pending": the app timed out waiting for the final RespPay. The money may still be moving. Status is resolved by ReqChkTxn or next-day recon — never by guessing.

CHAPTER 5

UPI API Catalogue and Message Structures

UPI is XML over HTTPS with digital signatures. Every message has the same skeleton:

<upi:ReqPay xmlns:upi="http://npci.org/upi/schema/">
  <Head ver="2.0" ts="..." orgId="..." msgId="MSG123"/>
  <Txn id="TXN456" note="Chai" refId="..." refUrl="..." ts="..." type="PAY"/>
  <Payer addr="alice@okhdfc" name="Alice" seqNum="1" type="PERSON" code="0000">
    <Info> ... identity / rating ... </Info>
    <Device> ... app, os, geocode, ip ... </Device>
    <Ac addrType="ACCOUNT"> ... IFSC, acnum ... </Ac>
    <Creds><Cred type="PIN" subType="MPIN"><Data>encrypted block</Data></Cred></Creds>
    <Amount value="20.00" curr="INR"/>
  </Payer>
  <Payees><Payee addr="chai@ybl" .../></Payees>
  <Signature> XML-DSig over the whole message </Signature>
</upi:ReqPay>

The core APIs your switch will implement or consume:

APIPurposeWho handles it in your bank
ReqPay / RespPayThe financial transaction (debit and credit legs)Issuer + beneficiary switch
ReqAuthDetailsNPCI asks payer PSP for payer details/credentials (collect flow)PSP switch
ReqChkTxnQuery final status of a transactionAll parties
ReqBalEnqBalance enquiry (PIN-protected)Issuer switch
ReqValAddValidate/resolve a payee addressBeneficiary PSP
ReqListAccountList customer accounts by mobile number during onboardingIssuer switch

Correlation identifiers — burn these in: msgId (one per message), txnId (one per transaction, shared by all its messages), and RRN (customer/settlement reference). Dedupe on txnId; log all three on every hop.

CHAPTER 6

Security — PKI, Signing, and the NPCI Common Library

Two independent security layers protect UPI:

Layer 1 — Message security (switch-to-switch): every XML message is signed with the sending institution's private key (XML-DSig) and verified against certificates exchanged with NPCI. Transport is mutual-TLS over NPCInet. Keys rotate on a schedule; ReqListKeys fetches NPCI's current public keys.

Layer 2 — Credential security (customer-to-issuer): the UPI PIN is captured only inside NPCI's Common Library (CL) — a signed SDK embedded in every UPI app. The CL encrypts the PIN with NPCI's public key into a credential block. No TPAP, PSP, or NPCI node can read it; only the issuer's HSM (via NPCI re-encryption) can verify it.

sequenceDiagram participant CL as Common Library (phone) participant PSP participant NPCI participant HSM as Issuer HSM CL->>CL: Capture PIN on NPCI-drawn pad CL->>PSP: Encrypted credential block (opaque) PSP->>NPCI: ReqPay with credential block NPCI->>HSM: Translated PIN block (issuer key) HSM-->>NPCI: PIN verified: yes/no

Device binding establishes trust before any of this: the app sends an outbound SMS from the SIM, the PSP verifies the mobile number, and a device fingerprint is registered. The triple (mobile number, device, app) becomes the first authentication factor; the UPI PIN is the second.

Architect's rule: your switch must treat credential blocks as opaque blobs — never log them, never persist them beyond the transaction, and keep all PIN operations inside the HSM boundary. This is audited during certification.
Outcome: Trace how a UPI PIN travels encrypted from phone to issuer HSM without any intermediary being able to read it, and list every key pair involved.

CHAPTER 8

Error Handling, Timeouts, and Response Codes

A UPI switch is judged less by its happy path than by its failure handling. The response-code taxonomy:

ClassExamplesMeaningRetry?
Success00Approved—
Business declineZ*-style: insufficient funds, wrong PIN, limit exceeded, dormant accountCustomer/account problemNo (customer action needed)
Technical declineU*-style: switch unavailable, CBS timeout, signature failureSystem problemYes, or reversal
DeemedCredit deemed approved on beneficiary timeoutNPCI assumed successRecon must confirm

Timeout discipline: each API has a response-time budget (financial legs are seconds, not minutes). Your switch needs per-hop timers, and must answer NPCI within budget even when your CBS is slow — often by responding "technical decline" rather than staying silent.

The money-state decision table — for every failure point, know where the money is:

Failure pointDebit happened?Credit happened?Resolution
Issuer declines (bad PIN)NoNoClean fail, notify payer
Debit OK, credit declinedYesNoAuto-reversal to payer (RET) or credit retry
Debit OK, credit timeoutYesUnknownReqChkTxn; deemed rules; recon (TCC/RET)
Issuer timeout after CBS debitYesNoIssuer must detect via recon and reverse

Idempotency is non-negotiable: NPCI retries messages. Dedupe on txnId + message type; the second delivery must return the same answer without double-debiting. Design your transaction store around this from day one.

Classic bug: debiting in CBS, then crashing before sending RespPay. NPCI times out, payer sees "failed", but money left the account. Only a debit-vs-response recon catches this — build that report before you go live, not after the first complaint.
Outcome: Build the full decision table: for each failure point in the flow, what state the money is in, who detects it, and who reverses it.

What an issuer / acquirer / PSP switch actually does inside.
CHAPTER 9

Issuer Switch Internals — the Debit Leg

When NPCI delivers a ReqPay debit to your issuer switch, this pipeline runs in well under a second:

  1. Ingress: mutual-TLS termination, XML parse, schema validation, signature verification against NPCI certs.
  2. Dedupe: is this txnId already known? If yes, replay the stored response.
  3. Business validation: account exists, active, not frozen; per-txn / daily limits; velocity rules.
  4. PIN verification: hand the credential block to the HSM; get yes/no. Count failures (PIN retry limits).
  5. Risk check: inline fraud scoring (Chapter 15) with a strict latency budget.
  6. CBS debit: post the debit — either direct debit or hold-then-confirm, depending on your CBS.
  7. Respond: build, sign, and send RespPay; persist the final state.
stateDiagram-v2 [*] --> RECEIVED RECEIVED --> VALIDATED: schema+sig OK RECEIVED --> REJECTED: bad sig / dup VALIDATED --> PIN_VERIFIED: HSM yes VALIDATED --> DECLINED: HSM no / limits PIN_VERIFIED --> RISK_CLEARED: score OK PIN_VERIFIED --> DECLINED: risk block RISK_CLEARED --> DEBITED: CBS success RISK_CLEARED --> DECLINED: CBS decline RISK_CLEARED --> TIMED_OUT: CBS timeout DEBITED --> RESPONDED: RespPay sent TIMED_OUT --> RECON_QUEUE: verify + reverse DECLINED --> RESPONDED

CBS integration is your hardest interface: legacy cores speak ISO 8583 or proprietary APIs, have batch windows, and their timeout is not "no". Model TIMED_OUT as its own state that recon resolves — never map a timeout to a decline blindly.

Design principle: every state transition is an immutable, timestamped event. When (not if) a dispute arrives 3 weeks later, you replay the event log to prove exactly what happened.
Outcome: Design the issuer-side state machine with all failure transitions and the exact response code emitted from each terminal state.
CHAPTER 10

Issuer Switch — Credit Leg, Non-Financial, and the HSM

As beneficiary bank your switch processes the credit leg: validate the payee account (exists, active, creditable — NRE/PPF rules apply), post the credit to CBS, and respond fast — remember deemed approval punishes slowness by making you liable anyway. Credits can also arrive as reversals (payer refund) and credit adjustments from recon.

Mandate execution: for Autopay debits, no PIN arrives — the authorization is the mandate itself. Your switch verifies mandate state (active, within amount/frequency), then debits. Mandate lifecycle (create / modify / pause / revoke / execute) is a state machine you own.

HSM deep dive — every cryptographic secret lives inside a FIPS-certified Hardware Security Module:

HSM operationWhen
PIN block translation & verification (PVV/offset)Every debit, balance enquiry
Set/derive PIN verification valueReqSetCre (PIN set/reset)
Card verification (CVD) checkPIN set via debit-card factor
Key exchange: ZMK/ZPK hierarchy with NPCIKey ceremonies, rotation
Message signing keys (may live in HSM)Every signed XML

Understand the key hierarchy: a ZMK (zone master key) exchanged with NPCI under dual control encrypts the ZPK (zone PIN key) that protects PIN blocks in transit; internal LMKs protect everything at rest. Plan HSM capacity: each debit costs 1–3 HSM calls, and HSMs saturate before CPUs do.

Outcome: List every HSM call made during one payment, one PIN-set, and one mandate execution — and explain why mandate execution makes none for PIN verification.
CHAPTER 11

Acquirer / PSP Switch Internals

The PSP/acquirer side faces apps and merchants rather than NPCI-initiated debits. Its jobs:

  • TPAP-facing APIs: session/device binding, VPA management, pay/collect initiation, transaction history, mandate management. This API surface is yours to design — NPCI only standardizes the NPCI-facing side.
  • Payment initiation: build the signed ReqPay from the app's request + credential block; manage the async callback and push the final status to the app (webhook / push notification).
  • Merchant acquiring: onboarding (KYC, MCC assignment, VPA issuance), static/dynamic QR generation, intent link generation, settlement to merchant accounts, refunds (a refund is a new, reversed ReqPay).
  • Collect handling: when NPCI sends ReqAuthDetails for a collect against your customer, you notify the app, gather approval + PIN, and respond.

Business layer: zero-MDR on UPI P2M means acquiring is monetized via float, VAS (soundbox, analytics), and interchange on specific rails. Know the money flows even as an engineer — they drive what you must build (e.g. instant merchant settlement vs T+1).

Outcome: Design the full API surface a TPAP like PhonePe would need from your PSP switch, plus the merchant onboarding and webhook model for an acquiring product.
CHAPTER 12

Performance, Availability, and Switch Architecture Patterns

UPI does 15+ billion transactions a month nationally; a mid-size bank's issuer switch sees thousands of TPS at peak (New Year midnight, IPL finals, salary days). NPCI enforces response-time SLAs and publishes bank-wise technical-decline league tables — being at the bottom is a reputational and regulatory problem.

Architecture patterns that work:

  • Stateless processing nodes behind a load balancer; all state in a fast transaction store. Any node can process any callback (the RespPay rarely hits the node that sent ReqPay).
  • Async pipelines with back-pressure: ingress queue → worker pool → CBS adapter pool. Shed load by fast-declining when queues exceed budget, rather than timing out silently.
  • Bulkheads per dependency: isolate CBS, HSM, and risk-engine thread/connection pools so one slow dependency can't stall the whole switch. Circuit-break a sick CBS and decline cleanly.
  • Active-active across two DCs with synchronous-enough replication of the transaction store; NPCI connectivity from both. DR drills are NPCI-mandated, not optional.
  • Hot paths in memory: limits counters, dedupe cache, and mandate state in a replicated in-memory store (with durable write-behind) — the database is for durability, not for the per-transaction hot path.

A worked capacity plan (2,000 TPS peak, p99 < 1s): 2,000 TPS × 3 HSM ops → HSM cluster sized for 6,000 ops/s with N+1; CBS adapter pool sized by CBS's real p99 (say 300 ms → 600 concurrent connections); transaction store sustaining 10k writes/s (each txn = several state events); NPCI links sized for 2× peak with headroom for retries.

Reality check: your switch is usually not the bottleneck — the CBS is. Most of your availability engineering is about protecting NPCI SLAs from CBS weather.
Outcome: Produce a capacity plan for 2,000 TPS peak with sub-second p99, sized component by component.

CHAPTER 14

NPCI Certification and Go-Live

Certification is a project, not a formality — typically weeks of scripted test execution with NPCI observing and collecting evidence. Test-case categories:

  • Functional: every API you claim (pay, collect, mandates, PIN set, balance, status check) across account types and amount boundaries.
  • Negative: wrong PIN, insufficient funds, frozen accounts, malformed messages, bad signatures.
  • Timeout & exception: forced timeouts at each hop; verifying your TCC/RET and reversal behavior. This is where weak switches fail.
  • Security: credential-block handling, log inspection (no PINs/blocks in logs), key rotation drills.
  • Performance/DR: sustained TPS runs and DC failover drills for production readiness.

evidence pack] C --> D[NPCI approval + RBI intimation] D --> E[Pilot: limited customers,

Go-live strategy: start issuer-only with staff accounts, then a capped pilot, then ramp. Keep a war-room for the first weeks: watch technical-decline rate, deemed transactions, and recon breaks daily.

It never ends: NPCI issues circulars mandating new features (new rails, limit changes, API versions) with deadlines. Budget a permanent compliance backlog — a UPI switch is a product, not a project.

Phase 5 — Risk & Money Movement

The parts that make it a real banking system, not a demo.
CHAPTER 15

Fraud and Risk Engines

UPI fraud is dominated by social engineering (victim authorizes the payment) rather than credential theft — which shapes what a risk engine can and cannot do. Your engine sits inline on the debit path with a hard latency budget (≈50 ms) and answers: allow, decline, or step-up.

Rule families that earn their keep:

  • Velocity: count/amount per account, device, VPA, and payee over sliding windows (1m/1h/24h).
  • New-payee friction: lower limits or cooling periods for first payment to a new VPA.
  • Device & binding signals: new device, recent SIM change, recent PIN reset + large payment = classic account-takeover pattern.
  • Payee reputation: NPCI negative lists, internal mule-account scores, payee's incoming-funds fan-in.
  • Geo/behavioral: impossible travel, odd-hours patterns, amount z-scores vs customer history.
flowchart LR R[ReqPay debit] --> F[Feature fetch
counters, device, payee rep] F --> RU[Rules engine
hard blocks + limits] RU --> ML[ML score
gradient-boosted / online model] ML --> D{Decision} D -->|allow| CBS[Continue to CBS debit] D -->|decline| RC[Decline w/ risk code] D -->|review| Q[Post-txn review queue]

Engineering notes: features must be precomputed or cached (counters in memory, reputation refreshed async) — you cannot do joins at transaction time. Every decision is logged with feature values for model retraining and dispute defense. NPCI also mandates fraud reporting (to CPFIR) and consumes/publishes negative lists.

Example rule: "PIN reset in last 24h AND new payee AND amount > ₹20,000 → step-up or decline." This one rule blocks a large share of account-takeover losses.
Outcome: Design an inline risk engine that scores in <50 ms without blocking the payment path, including its feature store and decision-logging design.
CHAPTER 16

Settlement

UPI moves messages in real time but inter-bank money in batches: Deferred Net Settlement (DNS). Customers are debited/credited instantly by their own banks; the banks square up with each other several times a day through NPCI.

flowchart LR subgraph Realtime[Real time] A[Payer debited at Bank A] --- B[Payee credited at Bank B] end subgraph Cycle[Per settlement cycle] N[NPCI computes net position
per bank] --> S[NTSL settlement file] S --> RBI[Net debit/credit to banks'
settlement accounts at RBI] end Realtime -.transaction logs.-> N
  • Cycles: multiple per day; each transaction belongs to exactly one cycle based on its timestamp/cutover.
  • NTSL (Net Settlement) files: NPCI's statement of your net position per cycle — the input to your GL posting.
  • Cut-over handling: transactions straddling a cycle boundary are the classic source of recon breaks; your switch must tag each txn with its cycle deterministically.
  • GL posting inside the bank: customer leg hits the customer account in real time against a UPI settlement/suspense GL; the NPCI cycle then clears the suspense against the RBI settlement account. Suspense must return to ~zero every cycle — a non-zero residue is a recon break by definition.
  • Fees: interchange (where applicable), PSP fees, and NPCI switching fees ride on separate fee files/GLs.
Follow one rupee: debit customer → credit UPI suspense GL → cycle NTSL nets it → RBI settlement account moves → suspense cleared. If you can narrate this cold, settlement holds no surprises.
Outcome: Trace one rupee from customer debit through NTSL to GL entries, and design how your switch tags every transaction with its settlement cycle.
CHAPTER 17

Reconciliation and Dispute Management

Recon is the immune system of a UPI switch. Every day you perform three-way matching keyed on RRN:

flowchart TD SW[Switch txn log] --> M{Match on RRN
+ amount + direction} CBS[CBS postings] --> M NPCI[NPCI raw/settlement files] --> M M -->|all agree| OK[Matched — done] M -->|mismatch| EX[Exception queues] EX --> TCC[TCC — credit confirmed late] EX --> RET[RET — return/reversal to payer] EX --> ADJ[Debit/credit adjustments]

The exception vocabulary:

InstrumentMeaningRaised by
TCC"Credit was actually given" — confirms a doubtful creditBeneficiary bank
RETReturn — credit could not be applied; money goes backBeneficiary bank
Credit adjustmentPost-facto credit to settle a breakEither side via NPCI
Debit adjustment / chargebackDisputed debit clawed back per rules and timelinesIssuer

Disputes: UDIR (UPI's online dispute resolution) lets customers raise complaints in-app; your switch must expose complaint APIs, auto-resolve what recon can prove, and respect strict turn-around times. Timelines are NPCI-mandated — missed TATs become penalties and RBI-reportable complaints.

Design rule: recon is a first-class subsystem — its own pipeline, dashboards, and exception-aging alarms — not a nightly script someone wrote in a hurry. Aged breaks are money you owe or money you've lost; both compound.
Outcome: Design the recon batch pipeline (file ingestion → matching → exception queues → adjustment posting) and the dispute workflow with TAT tracking.

Data design: an append-only, event-sourced transaction store is the natural fit — every state transition (Ch. 9) is an event; current state is a projection. Retain per RBI/NPCI audit norms (years, not months), with credential blocks never stored and PII encrypted at rest.

Build vs Buy, and Implementation Strategy

Most banks license a switch (established vendors offer certified UPI stacks); some build. The honest decision matrix:

CriterionBuy/LicenseBuild in-house
Time to marketMonths18–24+ months incl. certification
NPCI circular complianceVendor's treadmillYour permanent team
Differentiation (risk, UX, cost/txn at scale)LimitedFull control
Talent neededIntegration + opsPayments protocol + HSM + high-TPS engineering
EconomicsPer-txn/license feesHigh fixed, low marginal — wins at very high volume

If you build, sequence it:

  1. Months 1–3: simulator + issuer non-financial flows (heartbeat, list-account, PIN set, balance).
  2. Months 4–8: issuer financial debit/credit legs, HSM integration, transaction store, recon v1.
  3. Months 6–10 (parallel): risk engine v1, ops console, settlement/GL integration.
  4. Months 9–12: UAT with NPCI, certification prep, DR drills, performance hardening.
  5. Months 12–15: certification, pilot, ramp. PSP/acquiring side as phase 2.

Team topology: a core switch team (protocol + orchestration), a CBS-adapter team, a risk team, an SRE/ops team, and a compliance/certification owner. Testing strategy: NPCI simulator in CI, chaos drills on CBS/HSM dependencies, and replay testing from production message logs.

Outcome: A 12–15 month implementation roadmap with milestones, certification gates, and a defensible build-vs-buy recommendation for your bank's volumes.
